How much is enough when it comes to giving your kids a good life? Food, money, tech, opportunities?

It's a question I find myself asking constantly. I look at my two boys who think and act completely differently from one another, and wonder if I'm getting the balance right, or if I'm overcompensating for a past they will never have to experience.

### Growing Up on the Outside

When I was a kid, my family didn't have much money. We ate the cheapest, most basic food we could afford. My mum would take my dad's old clothes, cut them down and sew them back together to make something that fitted me.

We had no central heating. Our only source of heat was a single brick storage heater downstairs, which accumulated heat overnight and slowly released it throughout the day.

I remember waking up on cold winter mornings to frost on the inside of my bedroom window.

I wore the cheapest trainers available and played with toys bought from charity shops. At school, that kind of thing made you an easy target.

Lunchtime was a daily reminder of where I stood.

My friends would open neatly packed sandwiches filled with chunky ham or tuna, along with packets of crisps and chocolate bars. My lunch was almost always a strawberry jam sandwich, a blob of butter and a blob of jam squashed between two slices of bread and thrown into my school bag.

By lunchtime, it had usually become a soggy lump of jammy goo in the middle, surrounded by dry, crusty bread.

I wasn't given any lunch money either. Later, when my friends went to the corner shop for sweets or to the chip shop for lunch, I would sit and watch them eat. If something fell on the floor, I'd ask if I could have it.

At home, things weren't much easier. My dad found it hard to understand me, who I was, how I acted, and the things I would do. Feeling misunderstood at home and picked on at school, I learned to silo myself. I pulled inward and built my own quiet world.

### The Spark in the Dark

Yet that isolation, and the lack of material things, forced a different kind of growth.

As a kid, I loved playing with toy cars and whatever toys I had. They were rarely new. Some were bought with my weekly pocket money, initially 50p, later £1, while others were second hand hand me downs from neighbours or family friends.

Because toys were scarce, I treated them differently. I never smashed my cars into things or deliberately drove them through mud. I cleaned them, looked after them, kept their original boxes where I could, and lined them up neatly. When you don't have much, you learn the value of what you do have.

That same curiosity eventually moved beyond toys.

My dad was into music, and when he later came into a bit of money, he bought an Atari ST as part of his hobby. I was allowed to use it, and for me it opened up an entirely different world.

I was also lucky to have cousins who were into computers. As they moved on to newer machines, they passed their old ones down to me. First came a ZX Spectrum, and eventually an Amiga. At a time when I certainly couldn't have afforded computers like these myself, those hand me downs gave me access to a world I otherwise might never have discovered.

These weren't just computers to me. They were opportunities to explore, experiment and create.

I couldn't afford many computer games. So I had to find other ways to get the most out of what I had. I learned to experiment with restricted demo versions of games, figuring out how they worked and how I could modify them. I bought a second hand programming book from a charity shop and spent hours typing lines of code from computer magazines into the computer, just to make simple games like Snake.

I wasn't learning to code because someone told me it would be useful. I was learning because I wanted to make things.

If I couldn't afford something I wanted, my instinct became to figure out how to create it myself. I loved taking spare, old or broken parts and turning them into something new.

Looking back, that scarcity taught me more than simply how to go without. It taught me to value what I had, to be resourceful, to understand how things worked, and most importantly, to make rather than simply consume.

That was probably the beginning of the technical spark that still defines me today.

### The Modern Dilemma

Fast forward to today, and my approach to raising my own children looks entirely different.

I make sure my boys have decent clothes, name brands and, more often than not, the latest gear. When we're out and they ask for sweets or something they want, I get it for them, even though they receive £20 a month in pocket money alongside a monthly contribution to their savings accounts. The pocket money is meant for when they hang out with friends, but even then, I usually ask if they need extra cash before they head out the door.

But I often worry, am I spoiling them? Are they learning the value of a pound?

The truth is, my parenting choices aren't driven by a desire to indulge them. They are driven by protection. I never want them to sit at a lunch table feeling distinct from everyone else. I don't want them to be laughed at for cheap clothes, bullied for having throwaway items, or pitied by their peers. I want them to move through the world with a sense of security that I had to fight to build for myself.

### The Friction of Abundance

Yet that drive to protect creates a real internal conflict, and it hits each of my boys in completely different ways.

My youngest is naturally sensible with money. He saves, carefully considers what things cost, and if I offer to buy something expensive for him, he'll object, "No Dad, that's too much money." He's constantly looking for ways to earn his own keep, asking if he can wash the car or take on chores for a bit of extra cash. In many ways, he carries that innate respect for value without having had to live through what taught it to me.

My eldest, on the other hand, is the exact opposite. Money burns a hole in his pocket the second he gets it. He'll ask for something astronomical without a second thought, casually dropping questions like, "This YouTuber just got a £1,500 bike, can you get me one?" as if he were asking for a glass of water.

It cuts deep when I see brand new Nike Airs getting scuffed along the pavement, or a new hoodie tossed onto the dirty ground outside because they felt warm. To me as a kid, a pair of trainers or a clean top was something to protect at all costs. To them, abundance is just the baseline.

I want my boys to be respectful. I want them to look after what they have and understand the value of the money that bought those things. But it's hard to teach the value of a pound when you've made sure they never have to feel the lack of one.

When they ask nicely, or tell me "all my friends are getting one," the boundary softens because I still remember what it felt like to be the only one left out.

### Echoes in the Details

I see my past surface in the smallest, most unexpected rituals of daily life, even in making their school lunches.

I catch myself spreading the butter with exact precision, making sure it goes right to the very edges of the bread. I constantly check in to make sure they are happy with the fillings, ensuring it's neither too much nor too little. In a way my younger self never experienced, I am making sure their lunches are perfect.

### The Father I Want to Be

My relationship with my parents wasn't great, mainly because of the friction and challenges with my dad. That distance stuck in my mind, and it shaped the kind of father I wanted to become.

I deliberately try a different approach with my boys. I try to listen to them, see things from their perspective, and then help them understand mine as a parent. I keep in mind that they are still growing and learning. Things that matter immensely to an adult don't always carry the same weight to a kid, and I don't want to weigh them down with the anxieties of adult life before their time.

I want them to know I am always here for them, both as their parent and as their friend. I make it a point to remind them that I love them, and to actually verbalise and express that love out loud, something I rarely heard growing up.

### Finding the Balance

Parenting is a constant balancing act between preserving the lessons of your past and preventing your children from carrying your old wounds.

I know that my upbringing made me resilient, independent, and creative. It gave me my drive to tinker, build, and solve problems. But resilience shouldn't have to come at the cost of being the kid with the soggy sandwich, watching everyone else from the outside.

My goal isn't to force them into the same hardship I had, but to help them connect care with gratitude. I want to give them a stable foundation where they feel secure among their peers, while still learning to respect what it takes to provide that security.

I want them to step out into the world with confidence, carrying none of the old scars.
