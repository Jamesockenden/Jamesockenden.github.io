"Boss." "Manager." "Senior." "Leader." Companies are full of titles that imply authority over people. To me though, a formal title means very little on its own. Leadership isn't granted by an org chart, it is earned through integrity, transparency, judgement, and how you treat the people around you.

If I had to summarise my role as a Software Engineering Lead, I'd describe it through this metaphor:

I see my role as being a mountain guide. The team already has the skill, endurance, and expertise to reach the summit. My job isn't to carry them or push them from behind. It's to help choose a sensible route, make sure the path is clear of hazards, ensure everyone has the right equipment, and recognise when conditions have changed.

The team climbs the mountain. I help make the climb possible and get them to their destination.

Real leadership isn't about personal power. It's about enabling the people doing the work to succeed.

### The Myth of the "Interchangeable Engineer"

One of the most persistent misconceptions in management is treating software engineers like plug and play components, as though two Java developers are interchangeable resources that can simply be swapped when a project needs more capacity.

They're not. Every engineer brings a different combination of technical skills, experience, domain knowledge, problem solving ability, and communication style. Two engineers working on the same application may approach exactly the same problem in completely different ways.

One might be a deep technical implementer. Another might be particularly strong at architecture, stakeholder communication, or understanding the wider system. When those complementary strengths are recognised and combined, the result can be far greater than treating both people as identical units of capacity.

This is where the plug and play mindset can become particularly damaging.
When a deadline is at risk, the instinct can be to add temporary or contract resource and assume that more people will automatically mean more output. Sometimes that works. If you're dealing with a relatively isolated, modern application with clean boundaries and good documentation, a strong engineer should be able to contribute quickly.

But that isn't the reality of many long lived platforms.
Complex systems accumulate historical decisions, hidden dependencies, environment quirks, undocumented behaviours, access restrictions, and architectural constraints. Even well written code doesn't eliminate the context required to understand why the system works the way it does.

#### Onboarding has a cost

Someone new to the system needs help understanding that context, and providing that help usually comes from the people already carrying the delivery responsibility. For a period of time, the existing team's productivity can actually decrease before any additional capacity starts producing a return.

That doesn't mean temporary specialists have no value. They absolutely do. Nor does it mean teams should avoid bringing in new people.

It means we need to recognise a simple reality: Capacity and context are not the same thing.

We will always need new team members, contractors, specialists, and fresh perspectives. But unless the work is genuinely isolated from the complexity around it, people need time to understand the terrain before they can navigate it effectively.

### The Fetish of "Change" vs. Purposeful Evolution

We often hear sweeping statements: "We need to change," "Change brings efficiency," "If you don't change, you get left behind."

Continuous improvement is vital, but change for the sake of change is not good. Shuffling people between projects mid flight under the guise of "it's good to change" disrupts momentum and disorients teams. People stop, wonder why their focus was just shattered, and end up spinning around waiting for the dizziness to wear off.

When engineers challenge arbitrary change, management frequently dismisses their concerns as "resistance to change." If the goal is intentional upskilling and cross training, I am all for it but frame it honestly, plan for it, and adjust the delivery timelines accordingly. Change requires a real, objective reason not just a perceived one.

### Surviving "The Sandwich Effect"

Middle leadership in software engineering can be a deeply lonely place. You exist in a permanent pressure chamber sandwiched directly between upper management pushing directives down and engineering teams pushing technical realities up. On a single day, you can be balancing finances, line management, delivery deadlines, product value, system architecture, and quality standards.

I see part of my role as shielding the team from stress, but total isolation can backfire. So I try to be as transparent as possible, I brief my team on high level organisational shifts early, without dumping corporate anxiety on them, so they understand why priorities might fluctuate before something lands.

I’ve also realised that absorbing all top down friction alone isn't sustainable. While I never want to appear slopey shouldered, I try involve senior team leads in higher level conversations. It shares the load, builds their strategic insight, and prepares them for leadership without overloading them.

Together with the Product Owner and Solution Architect, we plan, prepare and prioritise work before it reaches the team. But when timelines are squeezed because upstream dependencies stall, pushing the resulting pressure onto developers doesn't fix poor planning further up the chain, it simply transfers the problem and puts unnecessary pressure on the people doing the work.

### Accountability Traps, Decision Logs, and Business Impact

Few things destroy trust faster than a manager who speaks strictly at a high level while ignoring the detail. It’s easy to say, "We should all eat healthily" everyone agrees in principle, but execution depends on budget, availability, dietary needs, and daily reality. In engineering, execution lives entirely in the detail.

We’ve all experienced the scenario where management mandates a specific technical path, ignoring warnings from technical leads that it doesn't fit the context. Months later, when the exact edge cases you surfaced cause issues, management forgets they forced the decision, asking why it failed and placing the burden back on you to fix it.

To counter this, I’ve started writing formal Decision Logs. Articulating objective tradeoffs in writing makes it clear why a path was chosen and what risks were accepted.

#### Building an effective team comes down to three human fundamentals

1. Reject "The Lie to Motivate"
Some leaders rely on manufactured optimism or false promises to keep morale high. It might buy temporary happiness, but the moment reality strikes, the lie is exposed and trust is permanently lost. Be honest with your team. Practice tact, hold back unverified facts to prevent unnecessary panic, but never lie to build fake motivation.

2. Step Aside and Give Credit Away
Unless I personally wrote the code or built the architecture, I use "we" or "the team." Real recognition means calling out individuals by name and stepping back so the people who did the work can present it themselves. My reward isn't taking center stage or being the loudest voice in the room to satisfy a corporate "visibility" checklist, my reward is seeing my team grow, succeed, and own their achievements.

3. Deconstruct "Underperformance" with Empathy
In my experience, engineers who genuinely "can't be bothered" are exceptionally rare. When someone appears to be falling short, its almost always traces back to three root causes:

- Expectations were never clearly defined.
- The work being done lacks visibility.
- The request itself is flawed or blocked by other issues.

Telling someone who is working at their absolute limit that they "aren't doing enough" is destructive. Check your expectations, make sure you are aligned, and above all, listen. Acknowledge their hard work and show appreciation first. People have feelings, and treating them with dignity is the only way to build a resilient, high performing team.

### Final Thoughts: Still Learning, Still Evolving

If there's one overarching truth about software engineering leadership, it’s that I am still learning.

Technology changes constantly, organisations evolve, and every project brings a different mix of people, capabilities, and challenges. What worked for one team might not work for the next. There is no static playbook for being a good leader.

This is precisely why I regularly ask my teams for direct, constructive feedback. What am I doing well? What am I doing wrong? How can I help? Real leadership requires the humility to acknowledge that you don't have all the answers, the willingness to adapt your approach, and the commitment to keep growing alongside the people you lead.
